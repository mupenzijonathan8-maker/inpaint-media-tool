Inpaint turns the tedious cleanup of a photo into a straightforward visual workflow: brush over the distraction, watch the background rebuild itself, and move on. The canvas stays front and center while the brush tool handles the heavy lifting, so removing a stray tourist or an unwanted object takes seconds instead of a full editing session. Every stroke is reversible, which keeps experimentation low-stakes and the process fluid. Fewer steps between the image in front of you and the one you pictured.

## 🔧 Requirements

- Windows 10 or Windows 11
- PowerShell 5.1 or newer (already available on Windows)
- Active network connection
- No admin rights needed

## How It Works

Inpaint works on a simple principle: what you mark disappears. Behind that simplicity sits a reconstruction engine that studies the pixels bordering your selection and rebuilds what was hidden, matching tone, texture, and gradient so the patch blends into its surroundings.

The workflow is built around the brush. Load a photo, size the brush to fit the object you want gone, and paint over it — the mask grows in strokes, giving you live visual feedback on exactly what will be removed. Preview the result before committing, and if a stray tourist, a power line, or a photobombing stranger vanishes without a trace, the undo command restores the original in a single click. Redo lets you experiment freely without consequence.

Zoom controls let you work at the pixel level when precision matters — tracing a thin antenna or the edge of a sign demands it — while full-image view keeps you aware of the whole composition. The tool never locks you into one pass: remove one object, inspect the filled-in background, refine the mask, and remove another. Batch processing extends the same logic across multiple photos when a whole set needs cleaning up.

The end result is a workflow that feels less like editing and more like erasing — mark what you don't want, and Inpaint reconstructs the scene as if it was never there.

## Workflow

Open your image in Inpaint and the canvas becomes a workspace for subtraction. The workflow is built around a single, satisfying loop: mark what you don't want, watch it vanish, refine the edge, and move on.

**1. Load the canvas.** Bring your photo into the editor. Everything you see is a candidate for removal — a stray sign, a photobombing stranger, a tangle of wires across a sky.

**2. Mask the distraction.** Reach for the brush and paint directly over the object or person you want gone. The mask grows with your stroke, so you can be loose and fast or tight and surgical. Adjust brush size as you go — wide sweeps for big intrusions, a fine tip for the tricky edges.

**3. Let the background rebuild.** Once the unwanted element is fully masked, the editor reconstructs what should have been there. Pixels from the surrounding scene flow inward to fill the gap, matching texture, tone, and gradient so the repair blends into the frame.

**4. zoom in and polish.** Zoom close to inspect the seam. If a sliver of the original object survived or the fill looks slightly off, add a touch more mask and run it again. The undo and redo controls let you test大胆 edits and step back instantly — experiment freely, because every pass is reversible.

**5. Repeat until the frame is clean.** Work through distractions one at a time or in clusters. Each removal is a small act of visual decluttering, and the image gets closer to the scene you actually remember.

**6. Export the result.** Save your finished photo in your preferred format and you're done — a cleaner composition with no trace of the edit.

The rhythm stays the same whether you're cleaning up one vacation snapshot or a whole folder: mask, rebuild, zoom, refine, save. Once the loop clicks, removing the unwanted becomes almost addictive — and every photo you touch comes out looking like it was always meant to be that way.

## FAQ

**How precise does my brushwork need to be before the magic kicks in?**

Less than you'd think. The brush is forgiving by design — rough, gestural strokes over the object are enough. Inpaint reads the surrounding texture, gradient, and grain, then rebuilds the gap as if the distraction was never in frame. Think of the brush as a highlighter, not a scalpel: mark the area, step back, and let the reconstruction do the heavy lifting.

**Will the filled-in area look patchy or smudged?**

The whole point of Inpaint is that the repair blends into the scene. Backgrounds with repeating patterns, soft skies, brick, grass, and water are where it shines — the rebuilt region inherits the color and rhythm of its neighbors. For trickier surfaces, a second pass with a smaller brush over any leftover edge tightens the result. Zoom in, sweep, zoom out, done.

**What's the smartest way to tackle a photo with several unwanted elements?**

Work from the biggest distraction to the smallest, and save between passes. Clear the obvious subject first, study how the background resettles, then mop up the smaller leftovers. Batch processing lets you queue a whole folder when the edits follow the same pattern — a stack of beach shots, a run of product photos — so the workflow stays visual and rhythmic rather than repetitive.

**Does removing an object damage the rest of the image?**

No. The brush only marks what gets rebuilt; everything outside your strokes stays untouched. Keep an eye on the undo history as your safety net, and export a copy before a big cleanup if you want to compare before-and-after side by side.

**Which image formats can I bring into a session?**

Common formats like JPEG, PNG, and BMP open without friction, so snapshots, screenshots, and scans all flow straight into the brush workflow.

**Can I put the results to work in client or commercial projects?**

Yes — edited images are yours to use for personal and professional work alike.

**Is it light enough for an older Windows machine?**

It runs comfortably on modest hardware — Windows 7 or newer, a couple of gigabytes of memory, and a sliver of disk space. The workflow stays snappy even on laptops that choke on heavier editors.

**What's the fastest way to learn the tool's rhythm?**

Open one photo, remove one object, and compare. The loop — brush, rebuild, review — teaches itself in a single pass, and every subsequent edit gets quicker as your eye learns where to place the strokes.
